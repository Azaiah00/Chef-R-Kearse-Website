# Chef R. Kearse — Twelve-Month Marketing Plan

Prepared for Chef R. Kearse, Private Chef & Catering. Richmond, Northern Virginia, Washington DC and Maryland.

---

## The one thing to do first, before any of this

**The Google Business Profile is not under your control, and the domain is not either.**

Everything in this document is worth less than fixing that. A local chef's Google listing is the single largest source of enquiries available to him — someone typing "private chef near me" or "wedding caterer Richmond" is a buyer with intent, and the listing is what decides whether they find you. Right now that listing points people at a dead website and is owned by somebody else.

So the order of work is:

1. **Recover the Google listing, or establish a new one.** There is a documented process for both paths in the handover notes. Start it this week; it takes time and the clock is the only part nobody can speed up.
2. **Settle the domain question.** Either recover it, or register a new one and move. A new domain costs about twelve dollars and can be live the same afternoon. Waiting months for a domain you may not get back, while the new site sits unlaunched, is the more expensive choice by a wide margin.
3. **Then run the campaigns below.**

Paying for traffic before step one is finished means paying to send buyers to a listing that will not convert them. Everything else here assumes those two things are resolved.

## What the programme is for

Three outcomes, in priority order:

1. **More corporate events and weddings.** These are the two lines with the highest value per event and the most room to grow, and they are the two the chef has said he wants more of.
2. **Fewer wasted hours.** Volume is not the goal. Qualified volume is. The intake form scores every enquiry and routes the weak ones to an automatic reply, so growth in enquiries does not become growth in unpaid phone calls.
3. **A list that compounds.** Every enquiry, every event guest, every person who downloads a menu joins an audience that can be reached for free next season. Over a year this becomes the cheapest source of bookings he has.

## The six campaigns

| Campaign | Goal | Who it reaches | When | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Corporate Holiday Parties | Eight corporate dinners, mid-Nov to end of Dec | Office managers, EAs, HR leads; corporate segment | Oct to mid-Dec | Paid budget + time |
| Wedding Season | Fill spring and summer Saturdays | Engaged couples; venue coordinators | Heaviest Jan–Apr, year round | Small paid budget + outreach time |
| Open Weekend Fill | Convert an empty prime date inside 3 weeks | Past clients and seasonal list, by region | Triggered by the diary | Free |
| Lapsed Client Win-back | Bring back guests dormant 6 months+ | Lapsed past clients | Monthly, small batches | Free |
| Seasonal Menu Drop | Keep the list warm; get forwarded | The whole seasonal list | Quarterly | Free |
| Review and Referral | Close the Google review gap | Every client after their event | Triggered, 3 and 30 days out | Free |

**Four of the six cost nothing but attention.** That is deliberate. A business this size should exhaust the free compounding channels before spending on paid reach, and the two that do cost money are seasonal rather than always-on.

## The calendar, month by month

**January** — Wedding season opens properly; couples who got engaged over the holidays start booking suppliers. Two wedding posts a week. Begin venue coordinator outreach: twenty venues, individually. Quarterly menu drop is not due, so the list gets the wedding content.

**February** — Wedding paid social running. Coordinator follow-ups and kitchen walkthroughs. Corporate is quiet, which makes it the right month to write next season's content.

**March** — Wedding season peak for enquiries. **Menu drop (spring).** Win-back batch.

**April** — Wedding enquiries still strong. First tastings from January enquiries converting to deposits. Begin building the corporate list for autumn.

**May** — Wedding deliveries begin. Every completed event triggers the review and referral sequence. Open-weekend campaign becomes the main driver as the diary gaps appear.

**June** — **Menu drop (summer).** Peak delivery month; marketing runs on automation while he cooks. Win-back batch.

**July** — Quiet for enquiries. The month to shoot photography properly — see the note below, it is the highest-return use of a quiet month. Open-weekend campaign works hardest here.

**August** — Autumn wedding deliveries. Start warming the corporate segment with content, not offers, so the October push lands on a familiar name.

**September** — **Menu drop (autumn).** Corporate campaign begins. Second, smaller wedding push for the following spring.

**October** — Corporate holiday campaign at full pressure: weekly emails, paid social, weekly posts. This is the month corporate budgets are decided; being early beats being good here.

**November** — Corporate holiday closing push. Honest scarcity on December dates. Win-back batch before the holidays.

**December** — **Menu drop (winter).** Delivering. Marketing on automation. Referral sequence firing after every event. Start January's wedding content now, while December's work is fresh.

## The segments

| Segment | Who is in it | What they get |
|---|---|---|
| Seasonal menu | Everybody who opted in, including enquiries that did not book | The quarterly drop, and nothing else unless they ask |
| Corporate | Anyone who enquired about a company event, plus corporate past clients | The autumn corporate sequence |
| Weddings | Engaged couples and coordinators | The wedding sequence and tasting invitations |
| Past client | Anyone he has cooked for | Review ask, referral ask, open dates, win-back |
| Lapsed | Past clients with no contact in 180 days | The win-back email, once, never twice in a quarter |

**One rule across all of them:** nobody receives more than two marketing emails in a month, and the seasonal drop counts as one of them. A chef's list is small and personal; over-sending it destroys the asset faster than any single bad email.

**The segment most people would delete:** enquiries that did not convert. Keep them. Someone whose dinner-party budget did not work this year gets promoted next year, and the seasonal menu costs nothing to send. Filtering hard at the front door only works if nobody is thrown away at the same time.

## What to measure, and what to ignore

**Measure:**

- **Cost per A or B band enquiry.** The portal scores every enquiry on arrival, so this is knowable within a day. A cheap enquiry that scores D is a cost, not a result.
- **Cost per booked event.** The only number that pays for anything.
- **Open prime dates still open at the end of the three-week window.** The real measure of whether the open-weekend campaign works.
- **Google review count.** Currently the biggest competitive gap. It should move every single month.
- **Bookings from people on the list more than six months.** The measure of whether the seasonal drop is doing its job.
- **Repeat rate.** A chef's business is built on second bookings. If it is not rising, nothing else matters much.

**Ignore:**

- Follower count. It is not correlated with bookings at this size.
- Open rates in isolation. A 60 percent open rate on an email that produced no enquiries is not a success.
- Click-through rate on paid social. It rewards curiosity, not intent.
- Impressions. Entirely.

**Where to cut first if the budget is tighter:** the wedding paid social. Coordinator outreach costs an afternoon each and will out-earn it.

**Where not to cut:** the photography. It is the constraint on everything else.

## The single highest-return thing on this list

**One day with a food photographer.**

Every image currently available is taken from his Yelp gallery and his Instagram, and nothing there is larger than about a thousand pixels on the long edge. That ceiling limits the website's speed score, limits the size any image can be used at, limits what can be printed, and limits every campaign in this document.

One properly lit day fixes all of it at once, and the same set serves the website, Instagram, the Google listing, the wedding directories and every email here — for years.

The shot list is in `ASSET-BRIEF.md`. The priority half of it:

1. Six to ten finished dishes, three-quarter and overhead, natural light, dark surface
2. Him working — hands, the pass, the carve, the plate going down
3. His face, properly lit, looking at the camera. The single most-used photograph a private chef owns
4. A dressed table before anyone sits down
5. One ten to fifteen second vertical loop for the site hero

Everything generated by AI in these campaigns is atmosphere only — a room, a texture, a surface, light. **No generated image ever depicts a dish a guest could order, or a wedding that did not happen.** That rule is absolute and is repeated on every prompt sheet, because a picture that misrepresents what arrives at the table is a false claim, not marketing.

## The upsell conversation, stated plainly

This programme is roughly forty hours of work a quarter to run properly: writing, scheduling, list management, coordinator outreach, reporting, and keeping the creative current.

It can be handed over as it stands — every email, brief, caption and prompt in this pack is finished and ready to use, and the portal's marketing tab queues the week automatically. He can run it himself.

Or it can be run for him. The honest case for that is not that he could not do it; it is that the forty hours a quarter is worth more to him spent cooking, and the campaigns only compound if somebody does them on schedule rather than when there is a quiet afternoon. A campaign run half the time produces less than a quarter of the result.

Either way, the creative is his.

## What is not in this plan, and why

**No influencer or gifted-meal campaign.** A private chef's credibility comes from the people who paid. A free meal for someone with followers produces content that reads as bought, and in a market this small the people who matter can tell.

**No discounting, anywhere.** Not on open dates, not for first-time clients, not seasonally. Discounting teaches the people who pay properly that the price is negotiable if they wait. The open-weekend campaign works on a specific date and a specific idea, not on a lower number.

**No invented urgency.** December Fridays genuinely go first, so saying so is true. A countdown timer on a chef's website is not, and one client who books and works that out tells others.

**No claims about awards, press, certifications, insurance or years of experience beyond 2018.** None of those are documented anywhere public. If any of them are true, they are among the most valuable things he could give us — a real award or a real press mention is worth more than a season of paid social.
